Voice AI at roughly the cost of text.

NavGurukul has built an open-source speech-to-speech layer that runs inside the browser on the laptops organisations already have. Voice synthesis happens entirely on the machine, and recognition can too. Any organisation can pick it up and build voice into what they already do.

Until now, that was the expensive way in.

Over 400 million Indians can't comfortably read a screen. Farmers, ASHA workers, daily-wage earners, first-generation learners. Every service built for them still arrives as text on a form, and the workaround is always the same: put a human in the middle, or leave them out.

Voice was the obvious answer and the priced-out one. At commercial rates, an organisation running 7,500 voice sessions would spend roughly ₹36 lakh on speech processing alone. More than simply paying people to do the work by hand. So voice stayed a demo, and never became infrastructure.

We hit this wall building learning tools for our own students, and spent a year engineering around it rather than accepting it.

How it works

Almost everything happens on the user's device. That single decision is what collapses the cost, removes the connectivity requirement, and keeps voice data private by default.

01

Audio is captured locally

The browser or app takes the microphone input. Nothing is uploaded.

02

Speech becomes text

Chrome's built-in Web Speech API recognises it at no cost. On Chrome 139+ this can run fully on-device.

03

Your model handles the conversation

Only text goes to the cloud. Plug in whichever LLM you already use. No lock-in.

04

Text becomes speech on-device

Piper TTS runs in-browser via ONNX Runtime, in an Indian-accent voice we trained.

05

Turn-taking is smoothed out

Voice activity detection and buffering keep pauses feeling like a conversation.

Nothing routes through our servers at any point. Speech synthesis is always local, and with Chrome's on-device recognition the audio stays on the machine too. That is what keeps DPDP compliance tractable, and what lets this survive a connection with two bars of signal. The speech layer adds under a second to a turn; how long the whole exchange takes depends on how fast your model answers, which in practice is two to three seconds.

What's actually been shipped

We'd rather show a small number that's real than a large number that's projected. Everything below is deployed and measurable today.

  • Open source
    The full speech-to-speech library is on GitHub and published to npm as speech-to-speech, under the GNU General Public Licence. Free to audit, fork, and deploy.
  • Indian-accent voice
    Our students called American-accented English "alien", so we trained a text-to-speech model on IISc's SPICOR dataset — 24,058 audio files, 49 hours — and published it on Hugging Face. It's the voice our own deployments use. The library also ships with the standard Piper voice set, so point it at our model rather than the default if you want the Indian accent.
  • Low-connectivity tested
    Deployed across 22 government schools in Dantewada, Chhattisgarh, on the kind of hardware and bandwidth those schools actually have.
  • 5,000 users
    In production across NavGurukul's residential campuses and the Zuvy learning platform, with performance holding steady as concurrency grew.
  • Nine years behind it
    NavGurukul has run residential tech campuses since 2016, placed 1,500+ alumni in jobs, and reached over a million school students through refurbished-device labs.

  • 3–4 weeks

    Other languages, on request

    We train and ship a new language or regional accent in three to four weeks from the point we agree on it. Bengali, Marathi, Telugu, Tamil, Gujarati and Punjabi are the ones on our development list. Name yours on the first call and it can be ready before your pilot opens.

  • In research

    Phones

    Mobile performance isn't good enough to launch and we won't pretend otherwise. Where it gets to depends on how local speech models develop over the coming months, so we're not putting a date on it. Plan your pilot around a shared computer.

  • Being tuned

    Turn-taking

    A word occasionally clips at a sentence boundary while the system works out that someone has stopped speaking. Knowing when a person has finished talking is a genuinely hard problem, and this is where our current engineering effort goes.

Integrating it, in seven steps

Realistically this is a few weeks of work with our team alongside you, not a weekend. Here's the shape of it so you know what you're agreeing to.

  1. Tell us who your user is

    One call. If voice isn't the right answer for your problem, we'll say so.

  2. Pick one workflow

    One task, one language, one group of users. Narrow scopes ship; broad ones stall.

  3. Run the sandbox

    Install the library, point it at your own model key, and hear it work in a test build.

  4. Set up language and voice

    Launch on English, or name the language you need and we ship it in three to four weeks.

  5. Wire it into your product

    Your team builds the interface. Ours joins the integration reviews and unblocks you.

  6. Pilot with real users

    100 to 300 people on the machines they'll really use. Measure completion, comprehension, latency.

  7. Scale, and send fixes back

    What you fix upstream makes the layer better for everyone else building on it.

What it runs on

Built for the laptop, which is the machine that sits in school labs, panchayat offices and field centres and gets shared by everyone who walks in. There are two ways to run it, and the difference is where speech recognition happens.

Standard setup

Recognition in the cloud, synthesis local
  • Chrome 25 or later, latest version recommended; Safari and Edge also work
  • A working microphone. A headset helps in a noisy room
  • Any modern laptop. A Core i3 with 8 GB of RAM is enough
  • Smoothest on Apple Silicon; runs well on mid-range Windows and Linux machines
  • Recognition streams audio to Google's speech service; the voice you hear back is generated on the machine
  • Nothing to install, no GPU, no server

Fully on-device setup

Everything local, nothing transmitted
  • Chrome 139 or later, or local WebAssembly models
  • 64-bit CPU with 4+ physical cores, 8 preferred
  • 8 GB of RAM minimum, 16 GB recommended
  • A GPU with 4+ GB VRAM helps, but a CPU fallback works
  • 300–800 MB of free disk for cached voice models
  • Works with no internet connection at all

Most partners start on the standard setup because it runs on hardware they already have, then move to on-device once they know which workflow matters. The application code is identical either way. Performance scales with the machine: synthesis is smoothest on Apple Silicon, and it runs well on mid-range Windows and Linux laptops.

Before you start

  • Chrome, Safari or Edge. On Windows, macOS or Linux. Firefox handles the voice output but not the recognition, so it isn't supported.
  • HTTPS. Microphone access needs a secure origin, so your site or app has to be served over HTTPS.
  • Two response headers. Speech-to-speech needs Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin and Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy: require-corp on your app. Worth checking early: they restrict third-party embeds and scripts, so an existing product may need adjusting before it can turn them on.
  • Microphone permission. Your users grant it once, in the browser, the same way any call app asks.
  • A language model key. Whichever provider you already use. Or ask us to run that side for you.
  • One technical owner. Someone on your team who can install an npm package and stays with the project.
  • No servers, no GPUs, no audio storage. There is no speech infrastructure for you to run or pay for.

Questions we get asked

What does it cost to use?

The layer is free and so is the help getting you live. We don't charge for integration, setup or support, because this was built with philanthropic funding to be shared infrastructure. Your only running cost is metered model usage, around $0.10–0.20 per hour of conversation, paid to your own provider. If you'd prefer we ran that side, we invoice at cost with no margin.

Do we have to credit NavGurukul?

Yes. Attribution is part of both the GPL and the terms of service: a visible credit wherever the voice layer is used, plus a mention when you write or present about the work. Attribution is how the next organisation finds this instead of paying $5 an hour for the same thing.

What data do you want back from us?

Usage data on how the technology is being used: sessions, minutes of conversation, languages, completion rates. Reporting it is part of the terms of service, because those numbers are what we take to funders to keep this free. You are responsible for telling your own users what is stored and shared and getting their consent before any of it is collected.

Which languages work today?

English in an Indian accent is the primary production language. Hindi is built and working, though responses take three to five seconds rather than two to three. Any other language or regional accent is trained on request and ships in three to four weeks, so it's rarely the thing that holds a pilot up. One note on setup: the library defaults to standard Piper voices, so you point it explicitly at our Indian-accent model to use it.

What hardware does it need?

A laptop running Chrome, Safari or Edge, and a working microphone. On the standard setup that's it, and a Core i3 with 8 GB of RAM is enough; it's smoothest on Apple Silicon and runs well on mid-range Windows and Linux machines. Running recognition fully on-device asks more: four or more CPU cores, 16 GB of RAM ideally, and a few hundred megabytes of disk for cached models. Phones aren't supported in V1.

Where does the voice data go?

Never to us. Text-to-speech always runs inside the browser, so the voice your users hear is generated on their own machine. Recognition depends on the mode: the standard setup uses Chrome's Web Speech API, which streams audio to Google's speech service, while Chrome's on-device recognition keeps it entirely local. Choose the on-device path when your users' audio must not leave the machine.

Are we locked into a particular AI model?

No. The layer handles voice in and voice out and is deliberately indifferent to what sits in between. Use whichever model you already have a relationship with, and swap it later without touching the voice layer.

How fast does it actually feel?

The speech layer itself adds under a second: recognition is effectively instant and voice synthesis takes a few hundred milliseconds once the model is cached. What a user waits for is mostly your language model, so a full turn typically runs two to three seconds in English. Keeping replies to two or three sentences makes the biggest difference to how responsive it feels.

Does it really work offline?

The speech layer does, completely. The conversation logic still needs to reach a model, so full offline operation means running a local model alongside it. We've built toward that with offline laptop deployments, and it's an active area of work.

Who owns what we build together?

You own your application and its content. The layer itself is GPL, so it stays open and anything derived from it inherits that licence. Improvements you make to the layer come back upstream. If the copyleft terms sit awkwardly with how your organisation distributes software, say so early and we'll talk it through.
